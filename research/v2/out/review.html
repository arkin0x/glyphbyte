Sixteen icons, four dots, one byte

Each glyph is a square frame holding one upright icon, which gives the first hex digit, and up to four corner dots, which give the second. That is 8 bits per glyph, the same as today, with no rotations and no filled shapes. Below is your prefix e8ed3798c6ff written in the draft.

e8ed3798c6ff
first hex digit

The icon alphabet

Eight icons are kept from the current set (pacman renamed pie and turned to face away from the moon). Eight are new, replacing shapes that were slow to draw or that looked too much like each other once rotation is gone. Every icon is upright, has a name you can say, and takes one or two pen strokes.

second hex digit

The corner dots

Read clockwise from the top-left corner: top-left is worth 8, top-right 4, bottom-right 2, bottom-left 1. Dots never touch the icon or the frame, so they stay separate blobs even in thick chalk.

worked example

Writing the byte e8

  1. Draw the square frame.
  2. First digit e: draw the star, upright.
  3. Second digit 8 is binary 1000: one dot in the top-left corner.
  4. Repeat for each byte, left to right, above one underline that starts with a fat dot.

Drawing times come from a simple model anchored on Google's Quick, Draw! medians: line 0.5 s, circle 1.4 s, triangle 1.9 s, square 2.3 s. A dot is assumed to take 0.3 s. These rank the options; a timed trial with real people would measure them. B and C are untrained because the set should be agreed on looks first.

evidence

What the research says

Lines and circles come first

Children copy a vertical line at 2, a circle at 3, a cross and a square at 4, a triangle at 5, a diamond at 6. Slanted lines are harder than level ones at every age.

Stanford pediatrics milestonesdiamond vs square, PubMed 3559475

Rotations and mirrors confuse people

Left-right mirrors are the most confused transform in children's letter tests, and 180° turns nearly as much. The current set spends 2 of its 8 bits on rotation.

Gibson et al. 1962Dehaene et al. 2010

Open vs closed is easy to tell

Even 4-year-olds tell a closed shape from one with a gap. The draft keeps closed icons (heart, star) apart from open ones (arrow, bolt).

Gibson et al. 1962

Count to four, no further

Up to about four items are counted at a glance and almost without error; beyond that, errors climb. Four corner dots stay inside that limit.

Trick and Pylyshyn 1994

Simple shapes, one stroke, seconds

In Google's Quick, Draw! data, a circle is drawn in about 1.4 s and recognised 97% of the time; a star in 3.1 s at 96%; an octagon takes 6 s.

Quick, Draw! dataset

Matoran: circle, lines, small circles

Every letter sits in the same outer circle and uses one to three lines and one or two small circles. Letters that differ only by where one dot sits (A, C, P, Q, U) are its weak spot.

Markers people can draw

d-touch and Artcodes read hand-drawn markers by counting separate blobs inside regions. They work because blobs never touch their region's edge, which is the rule the corner dots follow.

Artcodes, DIS 2017

Chalk and fields need big, simple marks

A QR code cut in a cornfield only scanned after the paths were re-tilled dark; circles survive camera tilt best because they stay ellipses.

your calls

Three decisions before training

  1. Direction. B keeps the character of your set and removes what made it hard to draw. C is the easiest to draw but has no personality. A stays as it is. My recommendation is B.

  2. The eight new icons. Tree, box, plus, flag, x, bolt, star and fish are proposals. Swap any. A replacement should be upright, nameable, one or two strokes, have no dot inside, and not be a turned or mirrored copy of another icon.

  3. Frame. A square gives the dots natural corners. A circle is quicker to draw and survives camera tilt better, but the icon would shrink to leave room for the dots. In the drawing-time model the square frame is the biggest single cost in B: 2.3 s of about 5.3 s, against 1.4 s for a circle.
